# Building a Better LLM Agent Harness: Techniques for Reliability, Cheap-Model Search, and Plain Output

## TL;DR
- The benchmark-vs-reality gap is real and measured: models memorize test sets, break when you change a name or number in a math problem, and degrade as context grows. Fix it in the harness, not the model — offload arithmetic to code (Program-of-Thoughts beat chain-of-thought 71.6% vs 63.1% on GSM8K), decompose long tasks into atomic steps with voting (MAKER ran 1,048,575 steps with zero errors), and keep the context window small and clean.
- Your cheap-generate / smart-select plan is well-supported. Verification is often cheaper than generation, so a weak model (Llama 3.3 70B) plus an ensemble of small verifiers reached 87.7% where the raw model was far lower, and DeepSeek V3.2 delivered 93% of Claude's capability at 11.6x lower cost. To get real diversity, force it explicitly (different-approach constraints, personas, island models, quality-diversity archives); raw resampling collapses to near-identical answers.
- Plain output can be engineered and checked. RLHF reward models favor length, which is why models pad and hedge. Prompting gives good *relative* control of reading level but poor *absolute* control, so wire a linter (Vale, proselint) plus a critique-and-revise loop (Self-Refine improved outputs ~20% absolute on average) into the harness as a rejection step, not a one-shot instruction.

## Key Findings

1. **Benchmarks lie in predictable ways.** A 445-benchmark review found weak construct validity across the field; 81.3% rely at least partly on exact-match scoring. Apple's GSM-Symbolic showed every one of 25 models drops accuracy when you only change numbers in a math problem. Context degrades output ("context rot") at every length increment, not just near the window limit.
2. **Reliability comes from architecture, not bigger models.** Extreme decomposition plus voting (MAKER/MDAP) and tool offloading (Program-of-Thoughts, PAL) both beat raw single-shot generation by wide margins. Per-step errors compound: 2% per step over 20 dependent steps is a ~33% failure rate.
3. **Cheap-generate/strong-select works when verification is genuinely cheaper than generation** — true for math, code, and anything with an executable check; false for open-ended prose where the judge is as fallible as the generator.
4. **Diversity must be engineered.** Best-of-N and self-consistency give most of their gains by N=5-10 and flatten by N=40, because extra samples repeat the dominant answer. Island models, MAP-Elites, and explicit "different approach" constraints produce genuine variety.
5. **Plain language is a measurable, enforceable property.** RLHF length bias explains the verbose register. Linters and readability metrics catch some of it; a critique-and-revise loop catches more. Neither is perfect — readability metrics don't distinguish scientific from lay language well, and prompting hits reading targets only in relative terms.

## Details

### 1. The benchmark-vs-reality gap

**Contamination and construct validity.** A systematic review of 445 LLM benchmarks ("Measuring What Matters," arXiv 2511.04703) found widespread validity gaps: the most common scoring metric was exact match (used at least partly by 81.3%, exclusively by 40.7%), which rewards surface memorization. Contamination — benchmark items leaking into training data — inflates scores even when developers act in good faith, because the community overfits to popular benchmarks over time (the "validation set reuse" problem). Andrej Karpathy and others have called this an "evaluation crisis." Construct validity is the question of whether a benchmark measures the capability it claims to; the review argues most don't establish this.

**Fragile arithmetic and reasoning.** Apple's GSM-Symbolic study (Mirzadeh et al., arXiv 2410.05229) built templated variants of grade-school math problems. Testing over 20 models on 5,000 samples, every model showed accuracy variance across equivalent instances. Changing only the *numbers* (not the logic) dropped accuracy; adding one irrelevant clause dropped it further. Their conclusion: model behavior "is better explained by sophisticated pattern matching" than formal reasoning, "so fragile... that changing names can alter results." Note one nuance the study itself raises: arithmetic on 3-4 digit numbers is fairly reliable — the failure is in *logical* robustness under perturbation, not raw calculation. This matters for harness design: you can't assume a high GSM8K score means the model reasons; you must verify each instance.

**Long-context degradation ("context rot").** Chroma's report ("Context Rot: How Increasing Input Tokens Impacts LLM Performance," by Kelly Hong, Anton Troynikov, and Jeff Huber, published July 14, 2025) tested 18 frontier models (GPT-4.1, Claude 4, Gemini 2.5, Qwen3) and found all of them grow "increasingly unreliable as input length grows" — even on trivial retrieval and copy tasks, and even far below the advertised window. This is distinct from the older "lost in the middle" effect (Liu et al., Stanford), where accuracy follows a U-shape and drops 20-30 points for facts in the middle. One controlled study found reasoning accuracy falling from 0.92 to 0.68 as inputs grew from a few hundred to 3,000 tokens. A Qwen-focused study found a "catastrophic" collapse: F1 ~0.565 in the stable region falling to ~0.302 at 50% of the 128K window — a 45.5% drop — even with the answer present. Implication: more context is not free; noise accumulation actively hurts.

**Error compounding over long horizons.** METR's work shows frontier models near 100% success on tasks a human finishes in under four minutes, falling below 10% on tasks over four hours. The math is simple and brutal: if each step has independent error rate p, an s-step task with no recovery succeeds with probability (1-p)^s. A 2% per-step error over 20 dependent steps is a ~33% failure rate, and the failures are usually irreversible. A long-horizon survey (Chen et al.) formalizes per-step error growing with attention dilution as context accumulates.

### 2. Harness design techniques that improve real-world reliability

**Offload what models are bad at.** Two 2022 papers are the core citations:
- **PAL (Program-Aided Language models; Gao et al., ICML 2023, arXiv 2211.10435):** the LLM writes Python for the reasoning steps and a Python interpreter does the arithmetic. PAL with Codex hit 72.0% top-1 on GSM8K, beating PaLM-540B chain-of-thought by absolute 15% — with a far smaller model. On GSM-HARD (large numbers) PAL reached ~61% where chain-of-thought collapsed to ~20%. On Object Counting, PAL beat CoT by 23.7 points.
- **Program-of-Thoughts (PoT; Chen et al., TMLR 2023, arXiv 2211.12588):** similar idea, "disentangling computation from reasoning." PoT beat CoT on GSM8K 71.6% vs 63.1% (~+8.5 points), and on FinQA 64.5% vs 40.4% (~+24 points, because of large-number calculation). Average improvement over CoT ~12% across datasets.

*Design rule:* force tool use for anything numeric, symbolic, or stateful (counting, date math, unit conversion, exact string manipulation). Let the model choose the tool only when the task is genuinely open-ended. The cost of a wrong forced tool call is low; the cost of a hallucinated calculation is high.

**Verification and self-checking loops.** Prefer executable verifiers (unit tests, property-based tests, assertions, type checks, schema validation) over LLM judgment wherever a task has a checkable answer. When you must use an LLM judge, know its documented failure modes:
- **Position bias** (prefers first/second option; ~5-15% effect) — mitigate by running both orderings and averaging.
- **Verbosity bias** (prefers longer answers; ~10-20% effect) — length-normalize or penalize length in the rubric.
- **Self-preference / self-enhancement** (prefers its own family's outputs; ~10-25% effect) — use a judge from a different model family than the generator.
- **Style/sycophancy bias** (rewards confident tone even when wrong) — use explicit rubrics, separate correctness from style.

The foundational finding (Zheng et al., arXiv 2306.05685, NeurIPS 2023) was that "strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans." The same paper showed reference-guided grading cut a math-grading failure rate from 70% to 15%. But 2025-2026 follow-ups documented frontier judges exceeding 50% error rates on targeted bias tests. Treat LLM-as-judge as a calibrated instrument: validate against a human-labeled golden set, and use reference-guided grading whenever a correct answer exists.

**Context management.** Anthropic's guidance ("Effective context engineering for AI agents") frames context as a finite resource with diminishing returns; the discipline is "finding the smallest set of high-signal tokens." Concrete levers:
- **Compaction:** summarize the conversation near the window limit and restart with the summary. Anthropic pairs this with git commits as checkpoints and a progress file the agent re-reads after compaction.
- **Tool-result clearing:** drop raw tool outputs from history once used — "one of the safest lightest-touch forms of compaction," now a Claude platform feature. Notably, the "Complexity Trap" paper found simple observation *masking* (hiding stale tool outputs without summarizing) beat LLM summarization on coding tasks.
- **Structured note-taking / external memory:** the agent writes notes to files outside the window. A Letta benchmark found simple filesystem memory hit 74.0% on LoCoMo, beating a graph approach (Mem0) at 68.5% — "memory is more about how agents manage context than the exact retrieval mechanism."
- **Sub-agent isolation:** give each sub-agent its own clean context for a narrow task; an orchestrator aggregates. Anthropic's multi-agent researcher beat single-agent on complex research, but used up to ~15x more tokens than chat — a real cost.

**Error recovery and preventing compounding.** The highest-leverage move is atomic decomposition plus redundancy, which converts multiplicative error growth into exponential error *reduction*. If atomic tasks have error rate p and you take n samples and select by consensus, system error becomes roughly O(p^⌈n/2⌉). One worked example: 5% per-action error with five parallel samples and consensus voting drops system error to 0.11% — a 45x improvement. Also: log per-step success against horizon length to confirm you actually have a decay problem (a flat-high failure rate is a capability problem these patterns won't fix).

**Structured output / constrained decoding — a real trap.** Forcing JSON or a grammar guarantees valid syntax but can degrade the reasoning inside it. Multiple studies (Tam et al., 2024) documented 10-30% quality drops under hard structural constraints. The "Format Tax" paper (arXiv 2604.03616) found the degradation "enters at the prompt, not the decoder" — the instruction to format hurts more than the token masking — and it "disappears when reasoning is decoupled from formatting." Practitioners (BAML) report structured-output APIs give lower-quality responses than parsing free text. *Design rule:* let the model reason in free text first, then emit structure as a final step. Constrain the wire format, never the thinking.

**The MAKER framework and comparable approaches.** MAKER (Cognizant AI Lab, arXiv 2511.09030) is the strongest published result on long-horizon reliability. It solved a 1,048,575-step Towers of Hanoi problem with zero errors — the first system to clear a million LLM steps. Three ingredients:
1. **Maximal Agentic Decomposition (MAD):** break the task into the smallest possible subtasks — often one decision per agent — each with minimal context.
2. **First-to-ahead-by-k voting:** several agents attempt each step; accept the first answer that leads by k votes.
3. **Red-flagging:** discard outputs whose structure signals trouble (excessive length, bad formatting) before they vote, which cuts correlated errors.

The scaling analysis: required votes grow only logarithmically with step count while cost grows roughly linearly, whereas assigning multiple steps per agent makes cost grow exponentially. A key, cost-relevant takeaway from the paper: "state-of-the-art reasoning models are not required; relatively small non-reasoning models suffice" — they used gpt-4.1-mini as the sweet spot. A parallel "Six Sigma Agent" framework (Jan 2026) reports the same O(p^⌈n/2⌉) math, reaching 3.4 defects per million with 13 parallel agents. Caveat: MAKER's headline result is on a highly structured, verifiable puzzle where each subtask has an exact answer; the paper's more general four-agent-type version (decomposition, decomposition-discriminator, solution-discriminator, solver) is described as "promising" but preliminary, so extrapolate to messy real tasks with care.

### 3. Multi-candidate / evolutionary approaches (cheap-generate, strong-select)

**AlphaEvolve and FunSearch.** FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., Nature 2023) pairs a frozen LLM with an automated evaluator, evolving Python functions and using an **island model** to preserve diversity: multiple sub-populations evolve separately, and the worst half of islands are periodically reset and reseeded from the best. This beat known results on the cap-set problem. AlphaEvolve (DeepMind, arXiv 2506.13131, May 2025) generalized it and made your exact cheap/strong split explicit: it uses "an ensemble... Gemini Flash maximizes the breadth of ideas explored, while... Gemini Pro provides critical depth." On 50 open math problems it matched the best known solution on 75% and improved on 20%. It is "the first method to find an algorithm to multiply two 4×4 complex-valued matrices using 48 multiplications," improving on Strassen's 1969 result of 49, and it improved the state of the art on 14 matrix-multiplication targets. DeepMind reported that using only a small base LLM "significantly degraded results" — the strong model matters for the depth step. Open-source implementation: **OpenEvolve**.

**Best-of-N, self-consistency, and how returns scale.** Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) samples N chains at temperature >0 and majority-votes. On GSM8K with PaLM-540B it lifted accuracy from 56.5% (single CoT) to 74.4% (N=40). But returns diminish fast: most gain arrives by N=5-10 and the curve flattens by N=20-40. The reason matters for your design: "additional samples often repeat dominant answer patterns rather than provide new reasoning evidence." Temperature should scale with N — around T≈0.4 is best at N=5, T≈0.8 at N=40 — because more samples can absorb more per-sample noise.

**Generator/verifier asymmetry.** This is the theoretical backbone of your plan. Jason Wei's "verification asymmetry" (2025): verifying a solution is often easier than producing one — true in optimization (dual certificates), factoring (checking is trivial), and code (running a test is easier than writing the algorithm). Stanford's **Weaver** (Saad-Falcon et al., arXiv 2506.18203, NeurIPS 2025) is the direct proof-point: it achieves "o3-mini-level accuracy with Llama 3.3 70B Instruct as generator, and an ensemble of 70B or smaller judge and reward models as verifiers (87.7% average). This gain mirrors the jump between GPT-4o and o3-mini (69.0% vs. 86.7%)" — a jump that normally requires heavy fine-tuning. They then distilled the verifier ensemble into a 400M cross-encoder that kept 98.2% of the performance while cutting inference compute 99.97%.

But the asymmetry is not universal. "Understanding Verification Dynamics" (arXiv 2509.17995) found verification success depends on interactions between problem difficulty, generator strength, and verifier strength — not verifier strength alone. And a Medium taxonomy notes that in modern agentic systems verification cost can actually *dominate* generation cost when checking requires reasoning about global system behavior. Rule of thumb: the cheap-generate/strong-verify split pays off most when you have an *executable* or *reference-based* check; it pays off least on open-ended prose, where the verifier is as fallible as the generator.

**Model cascades and routing.** These give measured cost savings:
- **FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia, Zou; Stanford 2023):** cascade cheap→expensive, escalating only on low confidence. Reported up to 98% cost reduction at matched quality on some benchmarks; 50-98% typical.
- **RouteLLM (UC Berkeley, ICLR 2025):** a learned router trained on preference data. Cut cost ~85% on MT-Bench while keeping 95% of GPT-4 quality, sending only 14% of queries to the strong model; ~45% savings on MMLU. Learned routers **transfer** across model pairs because they learn query difficulty, not two specific models.

Caveats you must design around: the escalation path is expensive (cheap cost + checker cost + strong cost > going straight to strong), so cascades only win when the cheap model succeeds ~85%+ of the time. There's a real failure story: a provider changed a cheap model's output format, a schema check started failing, and the router silently escalated ~90% of traffic to the most expensive model with no alert. Monitor your escalation rate as a live cost variable.

**Enforcing genuine diversity.** Raw resampling gives you near-identical answers (LLMs exhibit "mode collapse"). Techniques that produce real variety, roughly in order of strength:
- **Explicit "different approach" constraints and role/persona diversity** in the prompt — cheapest, surprisingly effective.
- **Temperature and prompt perturbation** — helps but limited; too high wrecks quality.
- **Island models (FunSearch)** — separate populations that can't cross-contaminate; a student replication found ~10 islands optimal for diversity vs compute.
- **Quality-Diversity / MAP-Elites** — the strongest formal tool. MAP-Elites (Mouret & Clune, 2015) discretizes a "behavior space" into a grid and keeps the best solution per cell, so you retain diverse-but-good candidates instead of collapsing to one. LLM-driven versions include **QDAIF** (Quality-Diversity through AI Feedback), **ELM** (Evolution through Large Models), and **LLMatic** for architecture search. For your harness, define behavior dimensions that matter (e.g., algorithmic strategy, time/space complexity, library used) and keep the best candidate per combination before the strong model selects.

**Cheap open models as the generation layer.** This is now well-supported by cost-accuracy data. A geospatial-agent benchmark (arXiv 2606.12821) found DeepSeek V3.2 delivered 93% of Claude Sonnet 4's capability (56.3% vs 60.8%) at 11.6x lower per-case cost ($0.011 vs $0.127) — "the strongest single-model recommendation for cost-sensitive deployments." The Pareto frontier ran Llama 4 Scout → Qwen3-235B → DeepSeek V3.2 → Claude Sonnet 4, and three of the four frontier points were open-weight. DeepSeek ships under MIT, Qwen under Apache 2.0, both self-hostable — but the hosted DeepSeek API runs on servers in China, a data-governance concern if you don't self-host. This validates your specific plan: DeepSeek/Qwen as the generation and voting layer, a frontier model reserved for the final selection or the hard "insight" steps.

### 4. Plain-language output

**Why RLHF models drift verbose and hedged.** The mechanism is documented reward hacking. Reward models exhibit **length bias**: they favor longer responses regardless of quality (Singhal et al., 2023, showed a length-only reward reproduces most of the RLHF gain). This drives the policy to pad. One example: standard GRPO training inflated average response length on Arena-Hard from 1,171 to 2,343 tokens. Reward models also favor particular formats (numbered lists, markdown tables) and confident register. So the "trying to sound smart" behavior you observe is a learned artifact of the preference data, not a reasoning necessity.

**Prompting reduces jargon but only in relative terms.** The important finding for your harness: prompting gives good *directional* control of reading level but poor *absolute* control. RephQA (arXiv 2509.16360) set four target grade levels and found "while models adjust readability in a relative sense, they fail in absolute alignment" — their Flesch-Kincaid grades "consistently fall short of the targets, with larger deviations at higher levels." It also found chain-of-thought prompting produces *less* readable text with no accuracy gain. MedReadCtrl (arXiv 2507.07419) corroborated: GPT-4, GPT-3.5, and Claude-3 show "increasing deviations as the requested readability level rises." An earlier study (arXiv 2309.12551) found ChatGPT achieved 87.5% Spearman correlation with target reading levels — good relative control. Bottom line: "write at a 9th-grade level" nudges the model in the right direction but won't hit a hard target, so you need a measurement-and-rejection step.

**Automated readability and plain-language checking to wire into the harness.** Layer these as linting/rejection gates:
- **Readability metrics:** Flesch-Kincaid Grade, Gunning Fog, SMOG, Automated Readability Index, Coleman-Liau, Dale-Chall. Cheap and fast. Limit: they only count syllables and sentence length; one study found they "may not effectively distinguish between scientific and lay language." Use them as a coarse gate, not a quality measure.
- **Prose linters:** **Vale** (syntax-aware, highly customizable, ships Google/proselint/alex/write-good/Readability packages and a Readability config that flags e.g. SMOG grade above 10), **proselint** (editorial advice), **write-good** (weasel words, passive voice), **alex** (insensitive language), **textlint**, **LanguageTool**, **Retext**. These catch hedges, weasel words, passive voice, and nominalizations mechanically.
- **Controlled-language standards:** **ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English** — an international standard (as of 2025) with 53 writing rules and ~900 approved words, each with one meaning and one part of speech. Rules: active voice, short sentences (≤25 words for descriptive, ≤20 for procedural), one instruction per sentence, no present-perfect tense, no dropped words. Built so non-native readers and, increasingly, downstream *agents* can't misparse instructions. There's an open ASD-STE100 Claude Code skill on GitHub (danyuchn/asd-ste100-skill) that repurposes it for agent-facing text. Caveat: Boeing's own Simplified English Checker notes "no language checker can guarantee full compliance... Only human writers can judge whether a sentence makes good sense" — linters catch mechanics, not meaning.

**Critique-and-revise loops for style.** This is your most powerful lever, and it targets style specifically. **Self-Refine** (Madaan et al., NeurIPS 2023, arXiv 2303.17651) uses one LLM as generator, feedback-giver, and reviser, iterating up to 4 times with no training. Outputs "are preferred by humans and automatic metrics... improving by ~20% absolute on average in task performance," and — directly relevant — several of its seven tasks are *style* tasks: Sentiment Reversal (rewriting tone/register, GPT-4 rose 3.8→36.2) and Code Readability Improvement. Its motivating example is literally rewriting a curt message into a clearer one. For your harness: generate, then have the model (or a different-family model) critique specifically for jargon, hedging, and abstraction against a rubric, then revise, then re-lint. **Constitutional AI** (Bai et al., Anthropic, 2022) uses the same self-critique-and-revise mechanism against a written "constitution," and found generating an explicit critique before revising beats revising directly — so make your style critique explicit, not implicit. (Caveat: CAI as published targets harmlessness, not writing style; you'd be extending the mechanism.)

**Caveats on the loop.** Self-critique has documented limits: "self-bias," where models favor their own outputs (Xu et al., 2024), and gains that can be inflated by task-specific feedback prompts (Huang et al., 2024). Mitigate with a cross-family critic (e.g., DeepSeek critiques Claude's prose) and a mechanical linter that can't be sweet-talked. Also mind the readability-accuracy trade-off: pushing for simpler prose can drop factual fidelity (arXiv 2511.05080, BLESS), so keep a correctness check downstream of the style rewrite.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Fix the reliability floor first (do these before anything fancy).**
1. Force tool use for all arithmetic, counting, date math, and state tracking. Adopt a PAL/PoT pattern: model writes code, interpreter computes. Expected: the ~8-15 point gains PAL/PoT show on GSM8K, larger on big-number tasks.
2. Add executable verifiers wherever a task has a checkable answer (tests, assertions, schema validation, type checks). Reserve LLM-as-judge for genuinely open-ended output, and when you use it, use a different-family judge, randomize option order, and length-normalize.
3. Decouple reasoning from formatting: let the model think in free text, emit JSON only as a final step. Don't wrap the reasoning in a grammar.
4. Manage context actively: tool-result clearing, compaction with a progress file and git checkpoints, external file memory, sub-agent isolation for sub-tasks. Instrument per-step success vs horizon length so you know whether you have a decay problem or a capability problem.

*Threshold to advance:* per-step success high and stable across your typical horizon.

**Stage 2 — Add the cheap-generate / strong-select layer.**
5. Use DeepSeek V3.2 or Qwen3 as the generation and voting layer; reserve a frontier model for final selection and for hard "insight" steps only. The 11.6x cost gap for ~7% capability difference makes this the default for high-volume steps.
6. Adopt MAKER-style atomic decomposition + first-to-ahead-by-k voting + red-flagging for any long, verifiable pipeline. Start with k=1 or first-to-ahead-by-2 and small non-reasoning models. Use consensus math (O(p^⌈n/2⌉)) to pick sample count per step from your measured per-step error.
7. For your evolutionary layer, adopt OpenEvolve/FunSearch's island model plus a MAP-Elites archive with behavior dimensions you care about (strategy, complexity, dependencies). Enforce diversity explicitly with "propose a genuinely different approach" constraints and personas — don't rely on temperature alone.
8. If you route rather than run all candidates, use a RouteLLM-style learned router or a FrugalGPT cascade — but monitor escalation rate as a live metric and alert if it spikes (the silent-90%-escalation failure is real).

*Threshold to advance:* verifier/selector agreement with a human golden set is high, and blended cost is below your target.

**Stage 3 — Wire in plain-language enforcement.**
9. Add a linting gate: readability metrics (Flesch-Kincaid, SMOG) as a coarse filter plus Vale with proselint/write-good/alex packages for hedges, weasel words, passives, and nominalizations. Optionally enforce an ASD-STE100-style rule subset for agent-facing text.
10. Add a critique-and-revise loop targeted at *style*: a cross-family model critiques for jargon, hedging, and abstraction against an explicit rubric, then revises. Reject and resample if the linter still fails. Keep a correctness check after the rewrite so simplification doesn't drop facts.
11. In system prompts, ask for concrete subjects, active verbs, short sentences, and a target reading level — but treat the prompt as a nudge and the linter+loop as the real enforcement, since prompting only controls reading level in relative terms.

*Threshold that would change the plan:* if readability metrics and human judgment diverge on your domain (they can for technical prose), downgrade the metric gate to advisory and lean on the critique loop and human spot-checks.

## Caveats

- **MAKER's zero-error result is on a structured, exactly-verifiable puzzle.** The million-step number is real but the task (Towers of Hanoi) has a checkable answer at every step. The generalization to messy, open-ended real work is described by the authors as preliminary. Treat extreme decomposition as proven for verifiable pipelines, promising elsewhere.
- **Generator/verifier asymmetry is conditional.** It holds for executable/reference-checkable tasks and can invert for open-ended reasoning, where verification cost may dominate. Don't assume a cheap check exists; build one.
- **Best-of-N and self-consistency plateau.** Beyond N≈20-40 you're mostly paying for repeated answers. If the base model is ~30% accurate on a task, more samples won't save you — change the approach.
- **Structured-output quality loss is real but mostly a prompt artifact**, and recent closed-weight models resist it. Test your own models rather than assuming.
- **Readability metrics are shallow.** They count syllables and sentence length, not meaning, and don't cleanly separate expert from lay text. Linters catch mechanics, not sense (Boeing's own tool says so). Use them as gates, not judges.
- **Self-critique has self-bias.** A model grading its own prose inflates its scores. Use a different-family critic and a mechanical linter it can't charm.
- **Some cited figures are single-benchmark or vendor-reported.** RouteLLM's 85% and FrugalGPT's 98% are specific to particular benchmarks and model pairs; treat them as proof the technique works, not as numbers you'll hit. Cognizant's MAKER and Chroma's context-rot report are the labs' own publications; the core findings are corroborated by independent work but the specific numbers are theirs.
- **DeepSeek data governance:** the hosted API runs in China. Self-host the open weights if data residency matters.
